01

The Teams

Two seasons headed in opposite directions. North Carolina hosts Syracuse in a classic sandwich spot: No. 1 Miami is at Kenan on October 31.

North Carolina (4-2, 2-1 ACC) hosts Syracuse (2-4, 0-4 ACC) at Kenan Stadium on Saturday, October 24, at 7:30 p.m. ET on the ACC Network. The opening number was Carolina by 9.5, and the Tar Heels are 4-2 against the spread to Syracuse's 3-3. This is the kind of game a program in Year 2 of a rebuild has to win if it wants the season to mean something: the Tar Heels have already matched last year's 4-8 win total through six games, two more wins would make them bowl eligible, and a visit from a winless-in-conference opponent is the softest remaining spot on the calendar. The next one is the hardest. Belichick's entire week is about making sure the Tar Heels don't look through this one.

North Carolina · 4-2

Better, but not good on offense

The 2026 Tar Heels are a better version of the 2025 Tar Heels in every way that can be measured, but they are still not a good offense. They opened with a 15-10 win over TCU in Dublin, a measure of revenge for the 48-14 loss in Belichick's college debut, then beat East Tennessee State 35-3. The first real test went the wrong way: UNC led Clemson 17-6 at halftime and lost 28-20, and a week after a bye, No. 3 Notre Dame came to Chapel Hill and won 37-26. What has made the team interesting is the last two weeks. UNC went to Pittsburgh and beat No. 25 Pitt 21-17, Belichick's first win over a ranked opponent at Carolina, then went to Duke and beat the Blue Devils 24-20 to bring the Victory Bell back to Chapel Hill. Both games were decided in the final minutes, and both were won by a defense that made plays when it had to.

The numbers describe an identity more than a juggernaut. Offensively, UNC averages 329.7 yards per game (117th nationally) and 23.5 points (99th). The passing game sits 89th at 213.5 yards per game, and the rushing offense is 120th at 116.2. Defensively, the Tar Heels allow 359.0 yards per game (75th) but only 19.2 points (34th), which is the kind of gap that comes from forcing field goals, blocking punts, and holding opponents to short fields. The glaring defensive weakness is third down: UNC is 113th nationally, allowing opponents to convert 43.3 percent. The glaring strength, and it is not a small one, is discipline. UNC averages 33.7 penalty yards per game, fifth-best in the FBS, a long way from the flag-heavy team that Belichick publicly blamed for losses last fall.

Syracuse · 2-4

Looks nothing like a 2-4 team

The Orange are 2-4 and the story of their season is the quarterback room. Syracuse has used five different quarterbacks through six games, started three of them, and enters Saturday with Steve Angeli (Achilles), Amari Odom (shoulder) and Malachi Nelson (a right-foot injury that knocked him out of the Louisville game, on top of bruised ribs) all dealing with injuries. Danny Lauter, a Georgetown transfer, is the next man up. Syracuse is one of four teams nationally to start three quarterbacks, and the only one where all three of those starters have thrown for 300 yards in a game, which tells you both how productive the skill players are and how little continuity the offense has.

The record reflects the chaos. After a 66-point opener against New Hampshire, the Orange lost to Cal, lost 27-13 at Pitt, survived UConn 42-41 in overtime behind five Odom touchdowns, lost 28-25 at Virginia, and lost 34-26 to Louisville last Saturday despite a 315-yard, two-touchdown day from Nelson that was undone by two interceptions and a fumble, all in the second half. Syracuse has now lost 11 straight ACC games and 12 straight against Power Four opponents, and one more loss on Saturday would tie the school record. The Orange have been outscored 78-36 in the second half this season, and three of their four losses have come by one score or fewer.

Statistically, Syracuse looks nothing like a 2-4 team. It averages 449.7 yards per game (29th), including 291.0 through the air (12th), and 31.7 points (52nd). The defense allows 331.3 yards per game (46th), 191.8 passing (43rd) and 139.5 rushing (64th), but gives up 25.7 points per game (88th). That disconnect is the whole Syracuse story: the yardage is respectable, and the points, the turnovers, and the penalties (98.0 yards per game, 138th nationally, against Carolina's 33.7) are where games are lost. Fran Brown, 14-14 in his third season after a 10-3 debut and a 3-9 follow-up, is dealing with special teams that have missed seven field goals and four extra points with two kickers and two holders already used.

Strength of schedule is the context that complicates the yardage comparison, and the gap is large. ESPN's current rating has Carolina's schedule 14th in difficulty and Syracuse's 64th out of 138 FBS teams (1 is hardest), which is a 50-spot difference.

What has actually been played fits the rating. Carolina has played No. 3 Notre Dame, No. 25 Pitt, Clemson and Duke, while each team has an FCS game on its ledger (East Tennessee State for UNC, New Hampshire for Syracuse). The one direct comparison is Pitt: UNC won 21-17 there, and Syracuse lost 27-13 there. It would be a stretch to say Syracuse has feasted on a soft schedule, because Pitt, Virginia and Louisville are real ACC opponents and the Orange are 0-4 in the league. But when Syracuse's 449.7 yards per game (29th) is set beside UNC's 329.7 (117th), the Tar Heels have been collecting theirs against the tougher road, which is a reason to read Syracuse's offensive numbers with some caution rather than throw them out.

The Series and the Stakes

UNC has beaten Syracuse three straight times, most recently 27-10 at the JMA Wireless Dome on October 31, 2025. That game gave Belichick his first ACC win. Carolina had 47 yards of offense before its first touchdown drive, trailed 10-6 at halftime, then scored 21 unanswered in the second half while Syracuse struggled to complete a pass until the second quarter. Demon June had his best game as a Tar Heel, with 182 total yards and two touchdowns. The setup this year is almost the reverse: Syracuse can move the ball, and UNC will try to shorten the game and win it with defense, special teams, and a few big plays.

Offense

Offensive coordinator Bobby Petrino is the primary play caller, and Belichick has leaned into his usual priority list: win the turnover margin, protect the quarterback, play the field-position game. UNC's best offensive moments this season have come in bursts (a 75-yard touchdown to Shamar Easter against Pitt, a blocked punt that set up a short touchdown against Duke) rather than through sustained drives.

The criticism after Clemson was that Petrino's second-half sequencing abandoned a run game that could have produced a 300-yard day. That is a real concern with a quarterback who is not a high-variance passer. The best version of this offense on Saturday runs June, McGill and Hall at a Syracuse front that has been strong in the first half and worn out in the second, and saves the shot plays to Shipp and Easter for play-action.

Defense

The Tar Heel defense is the reason this team is 4-2. It allows 19.2 points per game (34th nationally) and 359.0 yards (75th), and it has produced the game-deciding play in each of the last two wins: a late interception at Duke and a fourth-down deflection at Pitt. It also has two clear, repeatable flaws, which are stopping the run and getting off the field on third down (43.3 percent allowed, 113th). Both matter against an offense like Syracuse's.

Offense

This is the offense that should worry UNC, because it moves the ball. Syracuse averages 449.7 yards per game (29th nationally), 291.0 passing (12th), 158.7 rushing (66th) and 31.7 points (52nd), and converts 41.8 percent of its third downs (60th). Jeff Nixon runs a spread attack in his third year as coordinator, and Louisville and Syracuse combined for 669 first-half yards last Saturday. The problem is that Syracuse beats itself: the Orange commit 10.17 penalties per game, last in the FBS, give the ball away in bunches, and have scored 36 second-half points all season.

Defense

Syracuse's defense has improved sharply in yardage and not at all in points. First-year coordinator Vince Kehres came over from Toledo, where his defense ranked second nationally in total defense last season, and installed a 4-2-5 base with co-coordinator John Scott Jr. The Orange allowed 427.7 yards and 34.9 points per game in 2025 (124th and 128th), produced only 11 turnovers, and ranked among the bottom three nationally in sacks. This year they allow 331.3 yards per game (46th), 191.8 through the air (43rd) and 139.5 on the ground (64th), but still give up 25.7 points (88th). The yardage is better. The points are not, because the defense fades late and the offense's turnovers have kept giving opponents extra chances.

Billy Edwards Jr. is a sixth-year quarterback on his third school, and he has been exactly what Belichick said he wanted: a veteran who values what Edwards himself calls "mitigating risk." He began at Wake Forest as a 2021 redshirt, threw for 2,881 yards and 15 touchdowns against nine interceptions at Maryland in 2024, then lost almost all of 2025 at Wisconsin to a PCL strain in the opener. He beat Texas A&M transfer Miles O'Neill for the job in August, and the early returns are mixed. Against Notre Dame he went 17-of-28 for 208 yards, three touchdowns and two interceptions. At Pitt it was roughly 17-of-25 for 243 yards, two touchdowns and two more picks, and both interceptions were poor decisions, with 75 of his yards coming on one play. At Duke he was cleaner: 15-of-25 for 191 yards, a touchdown, no interceptions, and 20 rushing yards. He has also been sacked three times at Pitt and twice at Duke.

The pattern in the film is that Edwards is accurate on the opening script and much less reliable when the game stretches. He hit Shamar Easter for 75 yards at Pitt on a throw that traveled about 20 yards in the air, and he underthrew Jordan Shipp on a 49-yard play that should have been a much bigger gain. The offense has leaned on tight ends underneath, which reflects Edwards' limitations pushing the ball downfield.

Syracuse · Offense

A room in crisis

Angeli · Odom · Nelson · Lauter · no named starter

This is a position in crisis, and the answer on who starts Saturday is not public. Fran Brown has not named a starter, saying only that Angeli would practice Monday and the staff would see where it goes. Steve Angeli, the Week 1 starter, is still working back from the Achilles he tore in September 2025 and received a platelet-rich plasma injection the week of the UConn game. He completed 20 of 29 for 263 yards and three touchdowns against New Hampshire, then threw five interceptions across the losses to Cal (24-of-49, 306 yards, three picks) and Pitt (13-of-25, 161 yards, two picks) before Brown turned to Odom. Amari Odom replaced him and went 27-of-37 for 355 yards and five touchdowns, with another score on the ground, in the 42-41 overtime win at UConn, before a shoulder injury in overtime sidelined him. Malachi Nelson, a UTEP transfer, played the most recent two games: 16-of-29 for 217 yards and a touchdown at Virginia, and 26-of-39 for 315 yards, two touchdowns, two interceptions and a fumble against Louisville while taking five sacks. He left that game with a right-foot injury on top of bruised ribs. Danny Lauter, a Georgetown transfer, finished at Louisville (3-of-5 for 24 yards) and is the likely starter if Nelson and Odom cannot go.

The larger point is that all three starters have thrown for at least 300 yards in a game, which is a credit to the receivers and to Nixon's scheme, and none of them has been dependable at protecting the football. Syracuse has thrown at least seven interceptions in the five games before Louisville and added two more last week. If Lauter plays, the Tar Heels are facing a quarterback who has attempted only a handful of passes for Syracuse, in an offense that will probably be working from a simpler script.

The matchup

Against a Syracuse secondary that is gettable but opportunistic, the question is not whether Edwards can complete 65 percent of his passes. It is whether he turns the ball over in the one or two stretches where Syracuse's defense gets the game to feel loose. Davien Kerr is the one player in that secondary who has repeatedly turned a coverage snap into a turnover, and Edwards threw two interceptions each against Notre Dame and Pitt. A bad throw into the middle of the field with Kerr in the picture is how a seven-point swing happens.

On the other sideline, whoever plays quarterback is working behind a line that gave up 38 sacks last year and five against Louisville, and the Tar Heels have the pass rush to exploit that.

The backfield is a three-man rotation of Demon June, Jaylen McGill and Benjamin Hall, and it is the unit most responsible for the offense ranking 120th nationally in rushing at 116.2 yards per game. June, a sophomore (5-foot-11, 215), has five touchdowns, including the 4th-and-2 burst at Duke and the 1-yard score at Pitt, but he ran 13 times for 49 yards (3.8 per carry) at Duke. He was the best player on the field in the last meeting with Syracuse, with 182 total yards and two touchdowns, and the Orange know exactly what he looks like going full speed. McGill has been the more efficient back lately. He carried 12 times for 75 yards at Duke (6.3 per carry), including the 24-yard touchdown that won the game. Hall has been the least productive of the three lately: 12 carries for 25 yards at Pitt. The three were held to roughly 36 yards on 19 carries there, a poor performance, though a Pitt defense that ranks among the best against the run deserves some of the credit.

The encouraging data point is that UNC outrushed Duke 135 to 124 with Edwards adding 20 yards. The discouraging one is that the Tar Heels still do not have a back who consistently turns four-yard carries into seven-yard carries.

Syracuse runs a committee and has been without a clear lead back, in part because the 2025 rushing leaders left. Ahmad Miller, a Jackson State transfer who led the SWAC with 1,054 yards last season and is listed at 5-foot-10, 210 pounds, ran 18 times for 123 yards and a touchdown against Louisville. A fumbled handoff exchange between Nelson and Hill at the Louisville 5-yard line was one of the turning points in that game.

In the other direction, UNC's defense has been poor against the run, but Syracuse's 158.7 rushing yards per game is a long way from the 241 that Notre Dame piled up.

Jordan Shipp is the offense. The junior caught 60 passes for 671 yards last year and has been better this fall: seven catches for 109 yards against Notre Dame, six for 93 (with a 49-yarder) at Pitt, and 75 yards at Duke, plus the five-yard touchdown that was his first of the season and a costly fumble that Duke returned for a score. Shipp owned that fumble on the sideline, and he will likely draw Syracuse's best cover man on Saturday. Everything the Tar Heels do in the passing game is built around getting him the ball in space, and he is the most likely UNC player to bust a 20-plus-yard play.

Beyond Shipp, the passing game is a tight end operation. Jelani Thurman had two touchdowns against Notre Dame (including a 14-yarder with 21 seconds left in the first half) and six catches for 29 yards and a score at Pitt, while Easter is the vertical threat, a 75-yard touchdown against Pitt on his lone reception.

This is the best position group on the Syracuse roster, and it is where UNC's defense will be most tested. Umari Hatcher, a sixth-year receiver, is the best player on the offense. His seven touchdowns lead the ACC, he caught three touchdowns against UConn (the most by a Syracuse player since 2016) and he caught 13 passes for 224 yards and a score against Louisville, including a 65-yard gain and a 41-yard touchdown from Nelson in the first half. He is a deep threat who beats defenders on vertical routes and in jump-ball situations, and Nelson repeatedly found him behind the Louisville secondary down the right sideline.

The second piece is Calvin Russell III, the five-star freshman and the highest-ranked recruit the program has landed in more than two decades, who tore an Achilles in spring practice and debuted against UConn. He caught his first collegiate touchdown against Louisville (six catches, 53 yards) and promptly drew a 15-yard celebration penalty that forced a missed extra point, which says plenty about where the program is. Colorado State transfer Justus Ross-Simmons is a jump-ball weapon, Daunte Bacheyie and David Clement give Syracuse tight end options, and Cole Weaver, Elijah Moore and Darien Williams are all on the depth chart. UNC will have to decide how much help to give its corners over the top.

The matchup

This is where the Syracuse defense is vulnerable: its base is a 4-2-5 with just two linebackers, Chris D'Appolonia and Antoine Deslauriers, who usually lead the team in tackles but were not at their sharpest in the loss at Virginia. A seam from Easter or a middle-of-the-field route from Thurman against that structure is the most realistic way UNC gets chunk plays without asking Edwards to throw deep outside.

On the other side, Hatcher and Russell against the Tar Heel secondary is the matchup that decides how loose this game gets. Notre Dame's CJ Carr threw four touchdowns against it, and the Tar Heels cannot afford to wait for the late plays this time.

Syracuse hired Juan Castillo, a 28-year NFL veteran, to coach the line after allowing 38 sacks in 2025 (third most in the ACC and 12th most nationally), and the results have been uneven. The projected starters are Trevion Mack (6-foot-6, 320) at left tackle, Joe Cruz at left guard, Kahlil Stewart at center, Byron Washington at right guard and Kam Pringle at right tackle. Xavier Lewis has emerged as one of the better interior defenders in the country. The sophomore tackle (listed at 6-foot, around 250 to 260 pounds) has 28 tackles and 6.5 sacks, with two sacks in each of the first three games, and was an "all-down guy" in the 15-10 win over TCU. At Pitt he finished with 12 tackles, a half-sack, a tackle for loss and a pass breakup. When Lewis is in the backfield, UNC's defense looks like a Belichick defense. When opponents slow him down with doubles and chips, the pass rush is much more ordinary.

The edges have been less stable. Melkart Abou Jaoude, a 6-foot-5, 260-pound redshirt senior who led the ACC with 10.5 sacks last year, suffered a hand and wrist injury in the opener, missed the ETSU and Clemson games, and returned against Notre Dame. He is the most dangerous Tar Heel against Syracuse by history: in last year's 27-10 win, he had two sacks, six tackles and a forced fumble, which earned him ACC Defensive Player of the Week. Penn State transfer Jaylen Harvey (two sacks against ETSU), Richmond transfer Donovan Hoilette Jr. (6-foot-4, 245) and Smith Vilbert round out the rotation. The group has depth, but it has not been a dominant run-stopping front. UNC allowed 241 rushing yards on 45 carries to Notre Dame, 150 at Pitt (a poor first half that improved after halftime), and 124 at Duke, which leaves it 80th nationally at 147.3 yards allowed per game. Notre Dame beat it in the rain by running behind center Ashton Craig, and that is the template Syracuse's committee of Ahmad Miller, Tylik Hill and Ju'Juan Johnson will try to copy.

That is the best sign for Syracuse against a UNC rushing attack that ranks 120th nationally.

The back end is Syracuse's best defensive group and its most experienced. Demetres Samuel Jr. (who also returns punts), Chris Peal (47 tackles and seven pass breakups in 2025, a 77.2 coverage grade against Louisville), Braheem Long Jr. (53 tackles last year) and Cornell Perry all return, and Davien Kerr plays the nickel. Where UNC Should Target

Five places to hit Syracuse, in order of how much they should matter.

TARGET 01

The Syracuse quarterback and the pass rush

The single best place to attack is the position Syracuse cannot stabilize. Whoever plays, whether it is Lauter, a hobbled Nelson, a limited Odom or a not-quite-right Angeli, the Orange quarterback is playing behind a line that gave up 38 sacks last year, saw Nelson sacked five times by Louisville, and is going to see Xavier Lewis (6.5 sacks), Melkart Abou Jaoude (10.5 sacks last year, two against this team) and Jaylen Harvey on the same night. UNC does not need to blitz. It needs to win its one-on-ones and collapse the pocket, because Syracuse's quarterbacks have thrown at least nine interceptions in six games (five from Angeli in two games alone) and because the Orange fumbled an exchange at the Louisville 5-yard line. A hurried quarterback is the quickest route to short fields for Edwards.

TARGET 02

The run game against a two-linebacker front

Syracuse's 4-2-5 puts only two linebackers on the field, and the line in front of them faded after halftime at Virginia. UNC has a power-oriented line (Threatt and Lindberg are both listed at 315) and three backs who can each wear defenders down. The target is 30-plus carries and a feel for the second half. Syracuse's run defense is middling (139.5 yards per game, 64th), but Virginia's wildcat misdirection took advantage of its discipline and the Orange had no answer in the fourth quarter. UNC's offense averages 116.2 rushing yards per game (120th), and it needs to lean on a defense that has been outscored 78-36 after halftime. It is also the correct answer to the second-half play-calling that drew criticism after the Clemson loss.

TARGET 03

Tight ends and Shipp over the middle

With only two linebackers, Syracuse is thin in the middle of the field, and Easter and Thurman are the best-suited UNC players to attack that area. Easter's seam route for 75 yards at Pitt is the template, and a defense that will be concerned about Shipp on the outside is exposed over the top of its linebackers. Shipp will most likely draw one of Syracuse's perimeter corners, Peal or Samuel Jr. The goal is two or three explosive plays that flip field position rather than a high-volume passing day.

TARGET 04

Penalties, snaps and field position

Syracuse is last in the FBS in penalties and UNC is fifth-best in penalty yards, so the discipline gap is worth about a score by itself. The Tar Heels should also be aggressive in the kicking game. Syracuse has used two kickers, two holders and a long snapper who has been inconsistent, and a team that blocked a punt at Duke should rush every field goal and punt hard. If UNC gets even one block or one bad snap, the Orange offense is likely to spend the evening playing from behind.

TARGET 05

An early lead

The Tar Heels script their openers well: they scored first against Notre Dame, built a 17-0 lead at Duke, and led Clemson 17-6 at halftime, though they trailed at the break at Pitt after being outgained 213-98 in the first half. Syracuse has already been outscored 78-36 in second halves, and its quarterbacks are more likely to make mistakes when forced to throw to catch up. If UNC gets out to a two-score lead by halftime, the game script favors Belichick's team.

Where UNC Should Be Concerned

Six ways this goes sideways, led by the one that needs the least help.

CONCERN 01

Hatcher, Russell and the explosive play

The most dangerous thing about Syracuse is not its record. It is that all three Syracuse starters have thrown for 300 yards in a game, and the common denominator is a receiver group with real speed. Hatcher caught 13 passes for 224 yards against Louisville and has seven touchdowns, Russell is a five-star talent, and Ross-Simmons wins contested balls. UNC's secondary has been excellent at making plays at the end of games, but Syracuse can score quickly, and the Tar Heels allowed Notre Dame's CJ Carr four touchdown passes. A pair of 40-yard shots in the first half would change the shape of this game before UNC's run game can establish itself.

CONCERN 02

Third down and sustained drives

UNC's defense is 113th nationally on third down, allowing 43.3 percent conversions, and Syracuse's offense converts 41.8 percent (60th). Louisville converted six of seven third downs against the Orange defense, so the trend is not unique to UNC, but it matters because Syracuse's offense gains nearly 450 yards a game. If UNC cannot get Syracuse off the field, the defense will tire in the same way it did at Clemson, where the Tar Heels were outscored 22-3 in the second half by an up-tempo offense.

CONCERN 03

The run defense

UNC has allowed 241 rushing yards on 45 carries to Notre Dame and 150 at Pitt, and its 147.3 yards allowed per game ranks 80th. Syracuse does not have Notre Dame's offensive line, but it has Ahmad Miller (1,054 yards at Jackson State last year, 123 on 18 carries against Louisville) and Tylik Hill (140 on 28 carries at UConn) in a rotation that can wear out a front seven. If the Orange establish the run, the play-action game over the top to Hatcher becomes very difficult to defend, and Syracuse can put together the long drives that UNC has had trouble stopping.

CONCERN 04

Edwards and the turnover margin

Edwards threw two interceptions against Notre Dame and two more at Pitt, and even with a clean day from him at Duke, UNC lost the turnover battle 2-1, including Shipp's fumble that Duke returned for a touchdown. Syracuse's defense is a good bet to take advantage of those mistakes. Davien Kerr has two interceptions and a sack in a single game, and he showed against Louisville how quickly a throw over the middle can turn into a turnover. UNC can survive one turnover. It probably cannot survive three against a team that scores from anywhere.

CONCERN 05

The second half

This is the clearest weakness on the UNC side of the ball. At Clemson, UNC led 17-6 at halftime and lost 28-20, and the criticism of the second-half sequencing was aimed at Petrino. At Duke, UNC led 17-0 and trailed 20-17 late in the fourth quarter before McGill's 24-yard run. It has won two straight because the defense made plays at the end and a few big offensive plays landed, not because the offense controlled games. Edwards was sacked five times across the Pitt and Duke games, and Syracuse's pass rush with Johnson (13.5 sacks last year) and Adeleye can attack tired tackles.

CONCERN 06

The trap

No. 1 Miami comes to Kenan on October 31. Syracuse is one loss from tying the school record for consecutive ACC defeats, three of its four losses have been by one score or fewer, and Fran Brown has said his team is peaking. A team with nothing to lose and a dangerous group of receivers is exactly the kind of opponent that a team looking ahead to the No. 1 team in the country can underestimate. UNC has won two straight without playing particularly cleanly, and the margin for error is thin.
